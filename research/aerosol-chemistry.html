Aerosol Chemistry

Research theme · acidity, water and reactions inside atmospheric particles

How acidic are atmospheric aerosol particles, how much water do they hold, and how do those properties control the chemistry that happens inside them, especially during biomass-burning haze in Northern Thailand.

Overview

Aerosol pH and aerosol liquid water content are two of the most important and least directly measurable properties of atmospheric particles. They govern the partitioning of semi-volatile gases, the dissolution of metals, acid-catalysed secondary aerosol formation and the toxicity of the particles people breathe. Because they cannot be measured with a probe, they have to be inferred from composition with thermodynamic equilibrium models.

This theme brings my aqueous-chemistry background to the field: estimating aerosol pH and water content for real haze aerosol, testing how well current models represent it, and improving their treatment of pH-dependent processes. It is the focus of my postdoctoral research at the Environmental Science Research Center, Chiang Mai University, and new projects under this theme will be added here as they develop.

Why it matters in Northern Thailand

Every dry season, agricultural and forest fires blanket Northern Thailand in haze. Thailand has enforced a 60-day zero-burning period (February–April) since 2013, yet the problem persists. Source apportionment of 2021 PM2.5 in Chiang Mai (Chansuebsri et al., 2024) shows that biomass burning contributes up to 51% of PM2.5 during the smoke-haze season, when daily concentrations average 116 ± 35 µg m−3 and can exceed 200 µg m−3, far above the WHO guideline. On the worst days the sky turns orange-red, and the exposure is dangerous for public health.
