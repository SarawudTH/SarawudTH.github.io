Fe(II) oxidation kinetics across pH and temperature

PhD research · Imperial College London, 2021–2026

A rate law for the oxidation of ferrous iron by oxygen that works from strongly acidic, sulphate-rich solutions to near-neutral water, and across the temperatures found in the atmosphere.

Background

The oxidation of ferrous iron, Fe(II), by dissolved oxygen is one of the most pH-sensitive reactions in environmental chemistry. Near neutral pH it is fast and well characterised; in acidic solutions it slows dramatically, and the rate laws reported in the literature for very acidic, sulphate-rich conditions disagree with one another. Those conditions matter: SO2-derived sulphuric acid makes many atmospheric aerosols extremely acidic, and the iron they carry is a major source of bioavailable iron to the oceans. Acid mine drainage and other sulphate-rich waters raise the same question.

Part of the problem has been measurement. Glass pH electrodes misread strongly acidic solutions, so earlier studies either avoided the low-pH region or reported pH values that were themselves uncertain. My PhD set out to measure Fe(II) oxidation kinetics with a properly calibrated pH scale across the full range of interest.

What I did

I measured Fe(II) oxidation rates in oxygenated sulphuric acid solutions spanning pH −1.2 to 8.0 and 5 to 65 °C, using colorimetric Fe(II) analysis and electrodes corrected for acid error and temperature. From these data I derived a coupled pH–temperature logistic rate law that describes the apparent rate constant across the whole range with a small number of parameters. At low pH the rate becomes largely independent of pH but still increases with temperature; at higher pH the familiar strong pH dependence returns.

To separate the effects of acidity from those of the anion, I repeated the experiments in hydrochloric and nitric acid and combined them with literature data for perchloric acid. Fitting these together gave intrinsic rate constants for seven individual Fe(II) species, showing that ligand-bound species oxidise faster than the free Fe2+ ion. Density functional theory calculations then revealed a strong inverse linear relationship between the vertical ionisation energies of the species and their rate constants (R2 = 0.95), giving a physical basis for the empirical rate law.

Why it matters

The rate law can be dropped directly into atmospheric aerosol-iron models and into geochemical models of sulphate-rich waters, replacing extrapolations from neutral-pH data. The species-resolved constants and the ionisation-energy correlation offer a route to predicting oxidation rates in acid systems that have not been measured.

Methods and tools

UV–Vis colorimetry · dissolved O2 electrodes · PHREEQC · MINTEQ · HySS · DFT (Gaussian) · Python
