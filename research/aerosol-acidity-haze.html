Aerosol acidity and liquid water during biomass-burning haze

Current project · Chiang Mai University, 2026–present

How acidic are the particles in Northern Thailand's dry-season haze, how much water do they hold, and do our thermodynamic models get the answer right?

Background

Every dry season, from roughly January to April, agricultural and forest fires across Northern Thailand and neighbouring countries blanket the region in haze. Chiang Mai regularly records some of the highest PM2.5 concentrations in the world during these months. Beyond the mass of particulate matter, what happens chemically inside each particle depends strongly on two quantities that are rarely measured directly: the acidity of the aqueous phase (aerosol pH) and the amount of liquid water the particle holds (aerosol liquid water content, ALWC).

Aerosol pH controls which reactions can proceed inside a particle: the partitioning of semi-volatile species such as ammonia and nitric acid, the dissolution of metals such as iron, the formation of secondary organic aerosol through acid-catalysed pathways, and the toxicity of the particles people breathe. Yet pH cannot be measured with a probe; it has to be inferred from the particle's composition using thermodynamic equilibrium models.

What I do

At the Environmental Science Research Center of Chiang Mai University I collect size-resolved aerosol samples through haze and non-haze periods and analyse their inorganic ion composition. I then estimate pH and ALWC for each size fraction with the three thermodynamic models most used in the field, E-AIM, ISORROPIA and AIOMFAC, and compare how they behave for biomass-burning aerosol, which is rich in potassium, organic acids and ammonium rather than the sulphate–nitrate–ammonium mixtures the models were tuned on.

This project brings my PhD background to a field problem: having spent four years measuring and modelling aqueous chemistry at extreme acidity, I am interested in where the thermodynamic models are reliable for haze aerosol and where their assumptions break down.

Aims

  • Produce the first size-resolved aerosol pH and ALWC dataset for Chiang Mai covering both haze and clean periods.
  • Quantify how the choice of thermodynamic model changes the estimated pH for biomass-burning aerosol, and identify the chemical drivers of the differences.
  • Use the results to improve how these models represent haze-period aerosol, so that pH-dependent processes such as Fe(II) oxidation and metal solubility can be predicted more reliably.

Methods and tools

E-AIM · ISORROPIA · AIOMFAC · size-resolved sampling · ion chromatography
